The library opened early on a rainy morning. Nora carried a notebook and a small green umbrella. She wanted to understand why a simple model could describe a long queue outside the station. Every driver waited for a different amount of time, but the average pattern was almost a straight line.
At the next table, Leo arranged a set of colored cards. Each card held two numbers and a label. A straight boundary separated the first group of cards. The second group formed two circles, one inside the other. Nora drew several lines across the page. No single line separated those circles.
They built a small network with a hidden layer. The network combined several simple boundaries and learned a curved region. Training changed the weights a little at every step. The loss fell slowly at first and then more quickly. They checked the derivatives before trusting the result.
After lunch, the students turned to a collection of short sentences. They counted nearby words and stored the counts in a sparse matrix. Words that appeared in similar contexts had related rows. A decomposition compressed those rows into shorter vectors. The vectors preserved some patterns and lost others.
The word garden often appeared near flowers, trees, and rain. The word kitchen appeared near bread, bowls, and dinner. A vector could describe these associations without storing a dictionary definition. Nora reminded Leo that an association was evidence about the text, not a complete account of the world.
They used cosine similarity to compare the directions of vectors. Large counts alone were not enough to make two words similar. The students changed the number of retained dimensions and inspected the nearest neighbors. Some neighbors made sense. Others came from repeated expressions in the small collection.
In the afternoon, a friend asked for a movie recommendation. Nora wrote down a table of users and films. Most cells were empty because each person had seen only a few films. Filling those cells with zeros would confuse missing information with a poor rating. They trained a model using only observed entries.
The recommendation model assigned a short vector to every user and every film. A dot product measured their compatibility. User and film biases described broad differences in rating habits and popularity. A regularization term limited the size of the learned parameters. They compared the model with an average-rating baseline.
Leo wanted to recommend a movie that the friend had already rated. Nora removed previously seen films from the candidate list. They kept original movie identifiers so that every result could be matched to a title. They also checked how often a test film had never appeared in the training data.
Before leaving the library, they trained a recurrent model to predict the next character in a sentence. The hidden state carried information from earlier characters. Backpropagation sent gradients through the sequence in reverse order. Clipping reduced the effect of unusually large gradients during training.
The generated sentences contained familiar fragments and many mistakes. A lower training loss did not guarantee a meaningful story. The students measured the loss on a separate passage and kept the samples as an illustration of the model's limits. They wrote down the random seed and the training settings.
That evening, the rain stopped and the library grew quiet. Nora reviewed the figures while Leo checked the code. Their report connected each experiment to a matrix operation: a projection, an affine transformation, a decomposition, or a dot product. They saved the results so another reader could repeat the experiments.
